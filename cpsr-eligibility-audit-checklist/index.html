CPSR Eligibility Audit Checklist

CPSR eligibility verification is the process of checking whether a contractor meets the conditions for a Contractor Purchasing System Review. A CPSR eligibility audit checklist helps you confirm the right data, contract activity, purchasing volume, and system controls before a formal review is requested or scheduled. It also helps you spot gaps early so you can correct them before they create avoidable findings.

If you are trying to determine whether a purchasing system is ready for review, the best approach is to audit eligibility first and documentation second. That means confirming contract volume, contract mix, buying patterns, internal controls, and the evidence that supports each conclusion. A clear checklist reduces guesswork and makes the decision easier to defend.

  • Confirm whether purchasing activity is large enough to justify a review.
  • Verify that the contractor actually uses a documented purchasing system.
  • Check whether procurement records are current, complete, and traceable.
  • Identify any exclusions, exceptions, or special contract types that affect eligibility.
  • Prepare a file that shows why the organization is or is not a candidate for review.

CPSR eligibility audit checklist for determining whether the review threshold is met

The first eligibility test is whether the contractor’s purchasing activity is substantial enough to justify a CPSR. Auditors usually look for a pattern of meaningful subcontract and purchase order activity, not a one-time spike. The checklist should capture the volume, frequency, and dollar value of purchasing transactions across the relevant period.

Start by gathering a recent snapshot of purchasing activity, usually by fiscal year or trailing twelve months. The goal is to understand whether the company regularly places purchase orders, subcontracts, or other commitments that warrant formal oversight. If your data is fragmented across multiple systems, combine it into one summary before drawing conclusions.

A practical audit file should include total purchasing volume, number of purchase orders, top suppliers, and the types of buys being made. It should also note whether purchasing activity is concentrated in one program or spread across multiple contracts. A concentrated pattern may still support eligibility, but it needs to be documented clearly.

Do not rely only on dollar amount. A company with fewer but highly complex purchases may still need a review because the purchasing system itself carries risk. The checklist should therefore capture both scale and complexity.

CPSR eligibility audit checklist for confirming the purchasing system is actually in use

A contractor should only be evaluated for a CPSR if there is an operating purchasing system to review. The audit checklist should confirm that policies exist, that buyers use them, and that purchasing records show routine application. A written procedure without evidence of use is not enough.

Review the core purchasing policy set, including approval levels, competition requirements, source selection steps, and documentation standards. Then compare those requirements to a sample of actual purchase orders and subcontracts. The question is not just whether the rule exists, but whether the process followed the rule in practice.

Look for consistent recordkeeping. A usable purchasing file normally includes requisitions, quotes or bid notes, buyer notes, approval signatures, terms and conditions, and evidence of price or source rationale. If those items are missing across many files, the contractor may have a system problem even if purchasing volume is high.

It is also important to verify that the organization has staff who understand the process. If purchasing is handled informally by project managers or engineers without a standard workflow, the company may struggle to support eligibility conclusions. The audit checklist should identify who is responsible for buyer training, file maintenance, and purchasing authority.

CPSR eligibility audit checklist for mapping the contracts and suppliers that matter

Eligibility verification should focus on the contract and supplier profile that creates the review need. The most useful evidence shows who is being bought from, what is being bought, and whether the purchases are routine, commercial, sole-source, or highly controlled. That context helps explain why a CPSR is or is not appropriate.

Build a supplier map that separates routine vendors from critical subcontractors. Note which suppliers account for the largest share of spend and which ones support mission-essential work. This helps show whether the purchasing system handles a broad base of buying activity or a small number of special cases.

Also identify the contract types involved. If purchases support cost-type government work, they may require more careful control and documentation than commercial buying. Even when the company serves mixed markets, the audit should isolate the government-facing portion of the purchasing system so eligibility is not overstated or understated.

Check whether any supplier relationships are governed by special clauses, consent requirements, or flowdown obligations. Those details matter because they can change the level of purchasing oversight needed. A checklist that ignores contract structure will miss key eligibility signals.

CPSR eligibility audit checklist for testing file quality and evidence readiness

File quality is a strong indicator of whether the organization can support a CPSR. The audit checklist should test for completeness, traceability, and consistency across purchasing records. If a third party cannot follow the file from need identification to award decision, the system likely needs cleanup before review.

Choose a sample of purchase orders and subcontracts from different buyers, dollar ranges, and supplier categories. Then check whether each file contains the same basic elements. A strong file should let someone reconstruct the decision without asking for missing emails or informal explanations.

Common evidence gaps include missing approvals, undocumented source selection, late price analysis, and unsigned terms and conditions. Even one or two weak files can reveal a pattern if the same issue repeats across buyers. The purpose of the audit is to see whether gaps are isolated or systemic.

It helps to separate clerical omissions from control failures. A missing scanned document may be an administrative issue, but a missing competitive quote requirement points to a process breakdown. The checklist should flag both, but it should rank them differently so corrective action is targeted.

CPSR eligibility audit checklist for documenting exceptions, exclusions, and risk areas

Not every purchasing activity should be treated the same way in an eligibility review. The audit checklist should identify exceptions such as unusual supplier relationships, emergency buys, foreign sourcing issues, or contract-specific restrictions. Clear exception tracking makes the eligibility conclusion easier to defend.

Some purchases are not good indicators of purchasing-system readiness because they are unusual or nonrecurring. For example, a one-time facilities purchase may not reflect the normal control environment. Mark these items separately so they do not distort the overall eligibility picture.

Risk areas should also be documented. These often include weak segregation of duties, buyers who can both request and approve, inconsistent negotiation notes, and delayed supplier file closeout. Even if the organization is eligible for review, the checklist should show where the most likely findings will come from.

When risk is high, the best practice is to attach a short narrative explaining why. That narrative should name the control weakness, describe the affected transactions, and identify the corrective owner. This turns the checklist from a static form into a working management tool.

CPSR eligibility audit checklist for producing a defensible conclusion

A defensible conclusion ties the facts together in one simple statement. The final audit result should explain whether the contractor meets the practical conditions for a CPSR and cite the main records that support that conclusion. It should also note any limitations, such as incomplete data or pending corrective action.

Your final package should include a summary page, the supporting transaction sample, the purchasing policy set, and any exception log. If possible, add a short memo that states the basis for the conclusion in plain language. The memo should be understandable to procurement leadership, compliance staff, and outside reviewers.

A strong conclusion does not overstate certainty. If the data only covers one year, say so. If some purchasing activity sits outside the main system, identify it. Accuracy is more useful than a polished but fragile conclusion.

Used well, the checklist becomes a repeatable internal control tool rather than a one-time audit exercise. It helps purchasing teams know what evidence matters, what gaps to fix, and whether the organization is ready for formal scrutiny. That is the real value of eligibility verification: it turns a vague question into a documented decision.
